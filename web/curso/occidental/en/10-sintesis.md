# Module 10 — Synthesis: the whole picture

**Aim:** to write a complete, running reading of a chart. Not a list: a text.

## The idea

A chart is like a painting made zone by zone. Every planet, every house, every aspect adds a
stroke. But a heap of strokes is not a painting: **it has to be squared up**, and the one who
does that is the reader, not the chart.

That is where the craft lies. And also the risk, because that is the step where anything at all
can be smuggled in. Which is why synthesis needs method, not inspiration.

## The four-stage method

It is Margaret Hone's system, who ran the astrological school in London, and it is still the
cleanest there is.

**1. Literal translation.** Every factor, keyword by keyword, in stiff sentences. Deliberately
clumsy. The clumsiness is a defence: it stops you guessing.

> *"Your power and vitality express themselves assertively in matters of travel and deep study."*

**2. Conversational sentence.** The same idea, in decent English.

> *"You are an energetic person, keen to move, see the world and study seriously."*

**3. Group by category.** Every sentence is tagged in the margin and the ones on the same theme
are put together: character · mind · work and money · ties · family · health · leisure.

**4. Synthesise each group.** All the "character" sentences melt into one paragraph.
Contradictions are settled with module 9. You decide what is principal and what is shading.

## The order of reading

You do not start at the top of the list. You start with what rules:

1. **Ascendant and its ruler** — the general direction
2. **Sun and Moon** — identity and need, and the relation between them
3. **The dominant planet** (module 8) — who carries the voice
4. **The shape of the whole** — hemispheres, quadrants, stelliums, empty houses
5. **The configurations** — T-squares and the rest: the working point
6. **Everything else**, placed inside that frame

Details are read **inside** the frame, never loose.

## What has to be resisted

**Not everything goes in.** A chart holds hundreds of data points. A good reading uses maybe
thirty per cent and leaves out the rest. Putting it all in produces a flat text where nothing
stands out. Hone said it without hedging: you cannot expect to include every detail; you have to
choose and synthesise.

**No prefabricated paragraphs are pasted in.** Every reading is written whole for that person.

**No jargon with someone who does not have it.** The symbols go in the margin of your copy, not
in the text the other person reads.

## Questions

1. Why does the literal translation have to be clumsy?
2. You have forty sentences. How do you decide which go in?
3. Why do you start with the ruler of the Ascendant and not with the Sun?
4. What is the difference between a synthesised reading and a list of meanings?

## Exercise

The big one of the course: **write the complete reading of their chart.** Between 800 and 1500
words, running text, no lists, no jargon.

Working guide:
- Start from the clumsy sentences of module 5, which they already have
- Group them by category
- Apply module 9 to the contradictions
- Sort them by the order of reading
- Write

And a warning you have to give them: **they will want to like it.** It is their chart. Have them
ask themselves, sentence by sentence, whether they would write it the same way about a stranger.

## Closing question

**Who squares up the picture: the chart or the reader?**

The answer is the reader. And module 13 comes out of that, because if the reader is the one who
squares it up, the reader is also answerable for what it says.

## Common mistakes

- Handing over a list with bold headings and calling it a reading
- Putting everything in for fear of leaving something out
- Writing prettily before having translated literally
- Reading oneself with indulgence
